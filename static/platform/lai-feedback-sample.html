About Loss Aversion

What loss aversion is. Losing something often hurts more than gaining the same thing feels good. Losing $100 can feel worse than gaining $100 feels good. That idea is loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). It shows up with money, with wins and losses, and in other choices (Li et al., 2021). It is not the same as being risk-averse: loss aversion is about losses vs. gains, not about preferring safe vs. risky options (Li et al., 2021).

Three ideas in the chart. Reference point: Where you start from—often the status quo or “no change.” Diminishing sensitivity: The farther you get from that starting point, the less each extra gain or loss seems to matter (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Loss aversion: Losses feel bigger than equal-sized gains; the same amount hurts more than it pleases.

What higher scores can mean. You may pay more attention to what you could lose than to what you could gain. You may find it harder to let go of time or money you have already spent, and to try something new (Li et al., 2021).

Most people are at least a bit loss-averse. Spotting when it affects you can help you make clearer choices (Li et al., 2021).
